## Week 6 — AI Ethics: When Does Using AI Become Unethical?

### 1. Course Content
Students learn that AI ethics is a management decision problem, not a list of slogans. The week teaches students to distinguish ethics from law, compliance, and risk management; identify bias across data, measurement, design, and deployment; recognize privacy and confidentiality limits; evaluate transparency and explainability; assign human accountability; choose proportionate human oversight; verify AI claims; and identify stakeholders who receive benefits or bear harms.

The instructional sequence is:
1. Ethics vs. efficiency
2. Bias and fairness
3. Privacy and data ethics
4. Transparency and explainability
5. Accountability
6. Human oversight
7. Reliability, hallucinations, and verification
8. Stakeholder impact
9. Applied ethics decision labs
10. BrightPath Manufacturing case
11. AI Ethics Checker build

Students should leave Week 6 able to answer: **Should this AI use proceed, under what safeguards, with what evidence, and who is accountable?**

### 2. Key Terms
- **AI ethics** — the principles and reasoning used to judge whether an AI use is acceptable, fair, responsible, and aligned with organizational obligations.
- **Bias** — a systematic pattern that can create unfair or distorted outcomes because of data, measurement, design, or deployment choices.
- **Fairness** — the management requirement to examine whether benefits, burdens, errors, and opportunities are distributed appropriately across affected groups.
- **Privacy** — appropriate collection, use, access, sharing, retention, and protection of information about people.
- **Transparency** — meaningful disclosure of where and how AI is being used so affected people and decision-makers are not misled.
- **Explainability** — the ability to give understandable reasons for an AI-supported output or decision at the level appropriate to the audience and stakes.
- **Human oversight** — meaningful review by a person who has relevant information, competence, time, authority, and the ability to override or stop the process.
- **Accountability** — clear human and organizational responsibility for approving, using, monitoring, correcting, escalating, and stopping AI systems.

### 3. Student Build
Add an **AI Ethics Checker / Risk Assessment** to the personal AI Management Dashboard. The build must capture:
- AI use case and business purpose
- affected stakeholders
- data and privacy concerns
- possible bias or unfairness
- possible harms and who bears them
- transparency/explainability needs
- human-review requirement
- verification evidence required
- accountable owner
- risk level
- recommendation: proceed, proceed with safeguards, pause pending evidence, or do not use
- rationale and safeguards

### 4. Student Directions
1. Choose one realistic, non-confidential AI management use case.
2. State the business purpose before judging the tool.
3. Identify who benefits, who could be harmed, and who bears an error.
4. Identify every data source and classify privacy or confidentiality concerns.
5. Check for historical, representation, measurement, design, and deployment bias.
6. Decide what explanation affected people and decision-makers need.
7. Specify the human reviewer, the reviewer's authority, and when that person can override or stop the system.
8. Identify important claims or evidence that must be independently verified.
9. Assign a named accountable role.
10. Rate the risk and select a recommendation.
11. Write a concise rationale connecting evidence, stakeholders, risks, safeguards, and remaining unknowns.
12. Save the assessment in the dashboard and test that another person can understand the reasoning without additional explanation.

### 5. Example
**Use case:** AI ranks applicants for a customer-service role.

**Risk:** High.

**Recommendation:** Pause pending evidence.

**Reasoning:** Historical hiring data may reproduce earlier preferences, the vendor's "95% accurate" claim does not show false-negative rates or group performance, and automatic rejection would affect employment opportunity. HR should verify job-related criteria, compare outcomes across relevant groups, document candidate notice and appeal options, and require qualified human review before any applicant is rejected.

**Accountable owner:** HR Director.

**Safeguards:** No automatic rejection; documented criteria; fairness testing; privacy review; vendor evidence; human override; monitoring and stop condition.

## Week 7 — Responsible AI & Governance

### 1. Course Content
Students learn how organizations turn responsible-AI principles into repeatable management practice. The week distinguishes **policy from governance**, then introduces governance roles, decision rights, NIST AI RMF concepts (GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, MANAGE), data governance, risk treatment, deployment readiness, incident response, continuous monitoring, and governance maturity.

The instructional sequence is:
1. Responsible AI
2. Governance vs. policy
3. NIST GOVERN
4. NIST MAP
5. NIST MEASURE
6. NIST MANAGE
7. Operational AI policy
8. Data governance
9. Incident response
10. Continuous monitoring
11. Governance roles
12. Use-case mapping
13. Deployment readiness
14. Risk treatment
15. Governance maturity
16. Palmetto governance simulation
17. Responsible AI Policy build
18. Connect Week 6 risk to Week 7 controls
19. Cumulative Test 1 review and Test 1

Students should leave Week 7 able to answer: **Who decides, what control is required, what evidence proves the control works, what is monitored, and what happens when something goes wrong?**

### 2. Key Terms
- **Responsible AI** — the organizational capability to identify, evaluate, manage, document, and monitor AI risks while keeping humans accountable.
- **AI governance** — the system of roles, decision rights, policies, procedures, controls, evidence, monitoring, and escalation used to direct AI use.
- **AI policy** — written organizational rules and expectations for AI use; policy is one component of governance, not a substitute for it.
- **Governance owner** — the person or role responsible for a defined AI decision, control, monitoring duty, escalation, or stop authority.
- **Risk treatment** — a deliberate response to risk such as accept, reduce, transfer selected obligations, avoid, or escalate; accountability cannot simply be transferred away.
- **Incident response** — the process for detecting, containing, documenting, escalating, correcting, and learning from an AI-related problem.
- **Continuous monitoring** — ongoing review of performance, outcomes, controls, changes, and emerging risks after deployment.
- **Stop condition** — a predefined signal or threshold that requires pausing, limiting, or ending an AI use until the issue is resolved.

### 3. Student Build
Add a **Responsible AI Policy** to the personal AI Management Dashboard. It must include:
- organization and policy owner
- purpose and scope
- definitions
- approved AI uses
- prohibited AI uses
- high-risk uses requiring additional review
- confidentiality/data rules
- human-review requirements
- verification requirements
- disclosure/transparency requirements
- accountability roles
- security and vendor expectations
- incident-reporting/escalation path
- recordkeeping
- training
- monitoring
- review schedule
- version/status
- connection to at least one risk identified in Week 6

### 4. Student Directions
1. Reopen the Week 6 Ethics Checker and select one significant risk.
2. Define the governance objective: what must the organization prevent, detect, review, or correct?
3. Assign a business owner, technical/security role where relevant, compliance/legal reviewer where relevant, monitoring owner, escalation authority, and stop authority.
4. MAP the use case: purpose, affected people, data, context, assumptions, and possible harms.
5. MEASURE readiness: identify the evidence required for performance, fairness, privacy, reliability, and human review.
6. MANAGE the risk: accept, reduce, transfer selected obligations, avoid, or escalate; explain why.
7. Write operational policy language. Avoid vague rules such as "use AI responsibly." State who must do what, when, using what evidence, and what happens if the requirement is not met.
8. Define incident reporting and escalation.
9. Define monitoring, review frequency, change triggers, and stop conditions.
10. Connect the Week 6 risk to at least one Week 7 control and policy requirement.
11. Test the policy against the Palmetto simulation and revise language that is too vague to guide a real manager.
12. Save the policy in the dashboard.

### 5. Example
**Weak policy:** "Employees must use AI responsibly and check important work."

**Operational policy:** "Customer-facing AI-generated claims must be reviewed by the responsible business owner before publication. The reviewer must verify material factual claims against approved sources, document unresolved uncertainty, and stop publication when a material claim cannot be verified. The policy owner reviews this control quarterly and after any significant model or vendor change."

**Week 6 → Week 7 connection:** A Week 6 privacy risk becomes a Week 7 rule requiring approved tools, minimum-necessary data, access controls, retention limits, vendor requirements, an incident path, and a named owner.

### 6. Submission Requirements
Students submit or demonstrate:
- completed Responsible AI Policy
- named policy/governance owner
- purpose and scope
- allowed, prohibited, and high-risk-use rules
- data/confidentiality controls
- human-review and verification requirements
- accountability and escalation roles
- vendor/security expectations
- incident-response process
- monitoring and review schedule
- one explicit Week 6 risk → Week 7 control connection
- policy readiness evidence from the Palmetto exercise
- working dashboard evidence/screenshot or live demonstration

### 7. Rubric / Answer Key

### 8. Assessment — Test 1
There are **no Week 6 or Week 7 quizzes**. The only test in this two-week block is **Test 1 in Week 7**, cumulative over Weeks 1–7.

#### Test 1 blueprint
Recommended length: **30 items / 100 points / 45–60 minutes**.

Coverage:
- Week 1 — AI foundations, models/tools/LLMs, capabilities, limits, hallucinations, manager accountability: 4 items
- Week 2 — AI assistants, integrations/APIs, tool selection, privacy and safe data use: 4 items
- Week 3 — research questions, source quality, hallucinations, verification, knowledge building: 4 items
- Week 4 — AI productivity, workflow assistance, human review, disclosure considerations: 4 items
- Week 5 — decision support, missing information, alternatives, automation bias, human-in-the-loop accountability: 4 items
- Week 6 — ethics, bias/fairness, privacy, transparency/explainability, oversight, stakeholder impact: 5 items
- Week 7 — governance vs. policy, roles, NIST GOVERN/MAP/MEASURE/MANAGE, risk treatment, incidents, monitoring: 5 items

Question mix:
- 14 scenario-based multiple choice
- 8 concept/application multiple choice
- 4 matching/classification items
- 4 short management-judgment responses

The test should require application, not trivia. Students should have to decide what a manager should do and why.

#### Test 1 answer-key principles
Correct responses should consistently reflect:
- AI assists; humans remain accountable.
- A fluent or accurate-looking output still requires evidence and context.
- Sensitive or unauthorized data should not be entered into unapproved tools.
- Research claims require source verification.
- Productivity gains do not remove human-review requirements.
- AI recommendations must be checked for missing information and alternatives.
- Ethics analysis considers stakeholders, harms, fairness, privacy, oversight, and accountability.
- Policy alone is not governance.
- Governance assigns roles, controls, evidence, monitoring, escalation, and stop authority.
- Risk treatment does not transfer away the organization's accountability.
- High-impact AI requires proportionate evidence and meaningful human oversight.

The student-facing study guide may describe topics and practice scenarios, but it must **not reveal the actual test answers before submission**.
